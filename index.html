GEO 1102 · Semester Reflection

Learning to See
Beyond the Map.

A reflection on my learning journey in the Fundamentals of Geospatial Science and Technology, exploring how GIS, cartography, field observations and critical thinking have shaped my understanding of spatial information.

Amisha Sahay · MSc Geoinformatics
Looking back

More than a collection of maps

At the beginning of this course, I viewed maps primarily as visual representations of geographical information. Through practical exercises, thematic mapping, field data collection and the StoryMap assignment, I began to understand that creating a meaningful map involves much more than presenting locations. It requires appropriate data, thoughtful design, careful analysis and responsible interpretation.

The following reflections highlight the experiences that challenged my assumptions, developed my skills and influenced how I approach geospatial information.

Core Insights

Semester Reflections

REFLECTION 01

The Hidden Monsoon Jewel

Creating my StoryMap, "The Hidden Monsoon Jewel", helped me explore the relationship between Rugda mushrooms, Sal forests and the livelihoods and traditions of communities in Jharkhand. I combined geographical information, thematic maps and narrative elements to communicate a subject that connects ecology, culture and local economies.

This project made me realise that maps are not merely illustrations added to a presentation. Their spatial patterns, symbols, labels and extent can guide the reader towards a better understanding of a geographical issue. I also recognised the importance of connecting mapped information with reliable sources and acknowledging the limitations of the available data.

What changed? I began to see cartography as a way of communicating geographical relationships, not just displaying data.
REFLECTION 02

From Field Observations to Spatial Data

Working with field data collection introduced me to the importance of recording geographical observations systematically. Tools such as GPS-based data collection and Epicollect help connect observations to locations, making them useful for mapping and further analysis.

I learned that collecting a coordinate or entering an attribute is only the beginning of the process. Location accuracy, consistent recording, suitable attributes and verification are equally important. Errors or missing information in the field can affect the reliability of the final map and any conclusions drawn from it.

What changed? I became more aware that reliable spatial analysis begins with careful data collection and validation.
REFLECTION 03

Questioning AI-Generated Information

My assignment on mangrove loss and coastal vulnerability in the Sundarbans challenged me to evaluate information generated by artificial intelligence. I examined 12 claims against scientific literature. Six were supported, while six were only partially supported and required additional context or qualification.

This exercise taught me that an answer can sound convincing without presenting the complete scientific picture. Ecological processes are complex, and relationships between environmental change and ecosystem responses cannot always be explained through a single cause. Peer-reviewed research helped me distinguish supported evidence from claims that needed more careful wording.

What changed? I now understand AI as a supporting tool rather than a replacement for scientific evidence, verification and independent thinking.
REFLECTION 04

Learning Through Challenges

One of the most important lessons in learning geospatial techniques is recognising that a workflow or map is not automatically correct simply because the software produces an output. Coordinate systems, data quality, classification choices and map design all influence the result.

When I encounter a difficulty, I need to examine the data, review the steps I followed and check whether the result makes geographical sense. I also need to distinguish between what a map directly demonstrates and what I am interpreting from it. This is especially important when communicating environmental change.

My next step I will document errors, verify intermediate results and explain the reasoning behind my analytical and cartographic choices.

Looking ahead

How my perspective has changed

This course has helped me move beyond the idea that maps are simply finished products. I now understand them as representations built through a sequence of decisions about data, coordinate systems, scale, classification, symbology and interpretation. Each decision can influence the message communicated by a map.

I have also learned that geospatial technology is most useful when technical skills are combined with critical thinking. Whether I am creating a StoryMap, analysing environmental patterns or collecting field observations, I need to question the quality of the information, verify the results and communicate limitations honestly.

As I continue my MSc in Geoinformatics, I want to strengthen my skills in GIS, remote sensing, spatial analysis and cartography. Most importantly, I want to become more confident in using spatial evidence to understand real-world problems and communicate meaningful geographical insights.

My takeaway: A good map does not simply show where something is. It helps us ask better questions about why it is there, how it changes and what that means.
